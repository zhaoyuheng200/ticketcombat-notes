# What's changed since TicketCombat was written

Most of TicketCombat.com was written between 2008 and 2011, and much of it was
never revised. Ontario's ticket process has changed in several ways that
directly affect its advice. The changes below are checked against official
sources. Anything not listed may also be out of date. **Always follow the
instructions on your actual ticket and the court office or municipality named
on it.**

## 1. You can respond online, and the prosecutor meeting is "early resolution"

**What the site described:** going in person to the Provincial Offences court
office within 15 days to fill in a trial request form, often with a long wait
in line. It called the optional pre-trial meeting with a prosecutor **First
Attendance**.

**Now:** Ontario runs an online portal where, if your court offers it, you can
check a ticket's status, request an **early resolution meeting** with a
prosecutor, or request a trial. If the online option isn't available, you
contact the court office directly. The **15-day** rule still applies: if you
don't respond within 15 days, you're treated as not disputing the charge.

Source: [ontario.ca — Check the status of traffic tickets and fines online or request a meeting to resolve your case](https://www.ontario.ca/page/check-status-traffic-tickets-and-fines-online-or-request-meeting-resolve-your-case)
(last updated July 2026)

## 2. Toronto parking tickets no longer go to court (since Aug 28, 2017)

**What the site described:** a full parking-ticket defence built on the
Provincial Offences Act: requesting a trial, demanding disclosure,
cross-examining the parking officer, and Notices of Impending Conviction.

**Now:** Toronto handles parking tickets through its **Administrative Penalty
System (APS)**. You dispute through a **screening review** first, and can then
go to a **hearing officer**. There is no court trial. Several other Ontario
municipalities have also moved parking tickets to administrative penalty
systems, so most of the site's parking section doesn't apply there.
Administrative penalties also can't be looked up through the provincial
ticket portal.

Sources: [City of Toronto — Administrative Penalty System](https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/tickets-fines-penalties/administrative-penalty-system/),
[Penalty & Violation Screening Locations](https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/tickets-fines-penalties/penalty-violation-screening-locations/)

## 3. Toronto camera tickets moved to APS too (Jan 20, 2025)

Toronto red-light-camera and automated-speed-enforcement tickets with a
violation date on or after **January 20, 2025** go through the APS, not the
provincial court. Older camera tickets stay in the court system.

Source: [City of Toronto — Administrative Penalty System](https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/tickets-fines-penalties/administrative-penalty-system/)

## 4. Charter s.11(b) delay law was rewritten (R v Jordan, 2016)

**What the site described:** "stay" applications arguing that your trial took
unreasonably long, using the older *Askov* / *Morin* framework. That framework
weighed the length of the delay, its causes, any waiver, and prejudice to you.

**Now:** In **R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27**, the Supreme Court of Canada replaced
that framework with **presumptive ceilings**: 18 months for cases tried in
provincial court. Delay caused by the defence doesn't count toward the ceiling.
The site's 11(b) pages, precedents, and sample applications all predate
*Jordan*, so get current advice before relying on them.

Source: [R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 (CanLII)](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/2016/2016scc27/2016scc27.html)

## 5. Numbers, addresses, and statistics

The site quoted fine amounts, insurance-increase estimates, court and
prosecutor office addresses and phone numbers, office hours, and statistics
about how many tickets were thrown out. Treat all of them as historical. They
were accurate, at best, when each page was written.
