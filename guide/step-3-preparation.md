# Step 3 — Preparation

*A summary of TicketCombat.com's Step 3 section: 9 pages, written ~2008–2011,
Ontario. Not legal advice. Read the [original section introduction (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/20251017224226/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/step3.php).*

## What this section covered

Step 3 covered the months between requesting a trial and receiving the notice
of trial. Its central idea was that traffic trials often turn on memory and
credibility. A detailed account written now, backed by your own photos,
measurements and weather records, could make the defendant seem more reliable
than an officer recalling one stop among hundreds, and any reasonable doubt has
to go the defendant's way.

The first half is fieldwork. Revisit the scene from the officer's point of
view, look for blocked signs, ask whether the road layout allowed a valid radar
or lidar reading, and gather weather data. The second half is legal research. A
ticket involves two laws: the Provincial Offences Act sets the procedure, and a
separate statute (usually the Highway Traffic Act) defines the offence. The
*contrary to* section of the ticket tells you which provision to read for the
elements the prosecution must prove. Worked examples cover HTA s.145, s.128 and
the community safety zone provision (s.214.1), and cite R. v. Druce, 2006 ONCJ
183 on presuming that a posted sign is backed by a by-law. A resource page
points to CanLII and law libraries for case law.

The tone is upbeat and do-it-yourself. Claims about what a justice is bound to
do are best read as the site's view. The section originally ended with a First
Attendance page, which was later rewritten and moved to Step 2.

## Pages

### Step 3 introduction

`/step3/step3.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251017224226/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/step3.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site urged readers to spend the wait (up to two years, it said) reading
the rest of the site and, above all, writing a detailed account of the stop
while it is fresh. Its reasoning was that the officer must testify from
memory, not just read notes, so a defendant whose account is clearer and more
consistent can seem more credible and raise a reasonable doubt.

### Think outside the box

`/step3/outsidebox.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018075935/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/outsidebox.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site urged viewing the scene as a witness or justice would. Its example:
officers waiting around corners on Toronto's Bay Street can't confirm a
no-right-turn sign was unblocked when you turned.

### Look around, document the environment with different eyes

`/step3/lookaround.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251021154832/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/lookaround.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

Practical scene work: check signs for branches or snow, and take photos
yourself, since another photographer must come to court to vouch for theirs.
Large metal structures, dips and curves can, it said, undermine radar or
lidar, which must point along your line of travel. The site's example was a Peel police
speed trap on Cawthra Road in Mississauga under the Dundas overpass, a
low-accident stretch where it claimed over 80% of tickets are paid without
dispute. Satellite maps are weak evidence but can show distances, and simple
speed arithmetic (about 28 m/s at 100 km/h) can suggest a reading was taken
before you entered the posted zone.

### Weather is a factor

`/step3/weather.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018050638/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/weather.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site said to print weather for the offence date from two sources (it
named Environment Canada and Weather Underground) with the access date. It can
test the officer's memory, answer too-fast-for-conditions charges, and show wind
affecting a handheld lidar.

### How were you charged

`/step3/determine.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200803005327/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/determine.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-08

Every ticket involves two laws: the POA governs the charging, and another act
(the HTA or the Compulsory Automobile Insurance Act, say) defines the offence.
The site said to know both.

### The Provincial Offences Act

`/step3/poa.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018041723/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/poa.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site described the POA as regulatory law for everyday offences, less
formal than the Criminal Code, before justices and prosecutors it said are
rarely lawyers. It outlined Part I tickets, Part II parking tickets and Part
III summons matters, with a $1,000 maximum for Parts I–II (~2008); a footnote
flagged the Act's community framing as useful at sentencing.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** The description of requesting parking trials at the municipal office is dated for Toronto, where parking tickets have gone through the city's Administrative Penalty System since Aug 28, 2017 (other municipalities have adopted it too). Trial requests can now also be made through Ontario's online portal, where the court offers it. See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### Research the law you broke

`/step3/findact.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251019131554/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/findact.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The page showed how to read the *contrary to* notation on a ticket and look
the provision up on e-Laws or CanLII to find the elements the prosecution must
prove. For blocking an intersection (HTA s.145), it read the section's
exception as a full defence: a reasonable expectation of clearing before the
red was enough, cleared or not, and the site promised the judge "must find you
not guilty. Simple and easy!" For speeding in a community safety zone (HTA
s.128, with the zone created under s.214.1), it argued the higher fine depends
on a by-law setting the zone's hours and days, which the prosecutor must prove
with a certified copy and rarely discloses, opening the way to disclosure and
non-suit motions (Steps 4–5). It conceded that R. v. Druce, 2006 ONCJ 183,
lets courts presume a speed sign rests on a by-law, so this attacks only the
zone's extra penalty.

## Key takeaways

- Write down what happened right away. The site argued that memory and
  credibility decide many traffic trials.
- Go back to the scene, take your own photos, and collect weather and distance
  details that could test the officer's account.
- Identify both laws behind your ticket, the POA procedure and the offence
  section, and read the offence provision for its elements.
- Look for elements the prosecution might not prove, such as by-law proof for
  a community safety zone (which, the site conceded, targets only the zone's
  extra penalty). The site used these as groundwork for disclosure and
  non-suit arguments in [Step 4](../guide/step-4-pre-trial.md) and
  [Step 5](../guide/step-5-trial.md).
- Case law research starts on CanLII. The site's own case citations date from
  ~2004–2006.
