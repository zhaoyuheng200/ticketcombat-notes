# Step 3 — Preparation

*A summary of TicketCombat.com's Step 3 section: 9 pages, written ~2008–2011,
Ontario. Not legal advice. Read the [original section introduction (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/20251017224226/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/step3.php).*

## What this section covered

Step 3 covered the months between requesting a trial and receiving the notice
of trial. Its central idea was that traffic trials often turn on memory and
credibility. A detailed account written now, backed by your own photos,
measurements and weather records, could make the defendant seem more reliable
than an officer recalling one stop among hundreds, and any reasonable doubt has
to go the defendant's way.

The first half is fieldwork. Revisit the scene from the officer's point of
view, look for blocked signs, ask whether the road layout allowed a valid radar
or lidar reading, and gather weather data. The second half is legal research. A
ticket involves two laws: the Provincial Offences Act sets the procedure, and a
separate statute (usually the Highway Traffic Act) defines the offence. The
"contrary to" section of the ticket tells you which provision to read for the
elements the prosecution must prove. Worked examples cover HTA s.145, s.128 and
the community safety zone provision (s.214.1), and cite R. v. Druce, 2006 ONCJ
183 on presuming that a posted sign is backed by a by-law. A resource page
points to CanLII and law libraries for case law.

The tone is upbeat and do-it-yourself. Claims about what a justice "must" do
are best read as the site's view. The section originally ended with a First
Attendance page, which was later rewritten and moved to Step 2.

## Pages

### Step 3 introduction

`/step3/step3.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251017224226/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/step3.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site urged readers to spend the wait reading the rest of the site and,
above all, to write a detailed account of the stop while it is fresh. Its
reasoning was that the officer must testify from memory, not just read their
notes, so a defendant whose account is clearer and more consistent can come
across as more credible and raise a reasonable doubt.

### Think outside the box

`/step3/outsidebox.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018075935/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/outsidebox.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site asked readers to view the scene as a witness or the justice would,
not just from their own experience. Its example was the no-right-turn
restrictions on Toronto's Bay Street, where, the site argued, officers waiting
around the corner can't confirm that the sign was visible when the driver
turned.

### Look around, document the environment with different eyes

`/step3/lookaround.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251021154832/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/lookaround.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

Practical scene work: check signs for obstructions, take the photos yourself
(someone else's photos need that person in court), and consider whether metal
structures, dips or curves undermine a radar or lidar reading. It used a Peel
police speed-trap spot on Cawthra Road in Mississauga as an example (claiming
over 80% of those tickets are paid without a fight). It also showed how
satellite maps, though weak as evidence, and simple speed-and-distance
arithmetic can question whether a reading was taken in the right speed zone.

### Weather is a factor

`/step3/weather.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018050638/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/weather.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The site advised printing historical weather for the offence date from two
sources (it named Environment Canada and Weather Underground) and noting when
you retrieved it. The records could test the officer's memory in
cross-examination, address charges of driving too fast for conditions, and raise the
effect of wind on a handheld lidar reading.

### How were you charged

`/step3/determine.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200803005327/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/determine.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-08

A short page on the two-statute structure of a ticket. The POA governs how you
were charged, and a separate act, such as the HTA or the Compulsory Automobile
Insurance Act, defines what you allegedly did. The site said you need to
understand both to request a trial, defend yourself and know the possible
penalties.

### The Provincial Offences Act

`/step3/poa.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018041723/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/poa.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The page described the POA as regulatory law for everyday offences, handled
less formally than Criminal Code matters, before justices and prosecutors whom
the site said are rarely lawyers. It outlined the Act's three streams: Part I
tickets handed to you, Part II parking tickets, and Part III summons matters
with heavier penalties. It gave a $1,000 maximum fine for Parts I and II (as of
~2008), and a footnote flagged the Act's "community" framing as useful later
at sentencing.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** The description of requesting parking trials at the municipal office is dated for Toronto, where parking tickets have gone through the city's Administrative Penalty System since Aug 28, 2017 (other municipalities have adopted it too). Trial requests can now also be made through Ontario's online portal, where the court offers it. See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### Research the law you broke

`/step3/findact.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251019131554/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/findact.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

The page showed how to read the "contrary to" section of a ticket and look up
the provision on e-Laws or CanLII to find the elements the prosecution must
prove. In the first example, blocking an intersection (HTA s.145), the site
argued that a reasonable expectation of clearing the intersection is a
defence. In the second, speeding in a community safety zone (HTA s.128 with
s.214.1), it argued that the prosecution must prove the zone's by-law hours
applied, even though R. v. Druce lets courts presume an ordinary speed sign is
backed by a by-law (setting up disclosure and non-suit motions in Steps 4–5).

### Look for precedents

`/step3/precedents.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251018163944/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/precedents.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-10

A resource list for case research. It recommended CanLII (including decisions
from other provinces), Ontario's six law-school libraries and the Law Society
library, paid services such as Quicklaw, legal clinics, and public libraries as
a last resort (links as of when written). A later addition pointed to the
Ontario Court of Justice's guide for defendants in provincial offences cases,
which the site called a basic overview with no strategy.

### First attendance explained

`/step3/attendance.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20090220223619/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step3/attendance.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2009-02

This was the original version of the site's page on the pre-trial meeting with
a prosecutor. It took a harder line than the later version: refuse any deal,
admit and explain nothing, never ask whether the officer will attend, and don't
call your insurer to ask how much your rate would rise. The archive's last copy
is from Feb 2009, and the page seems to have been removed or merged into the
rewritten [Step 2 First Attendance page](../guide/step-2-request-a-trial.md#first-attendance)
(first seen June 2009), since later Step 3 sidebars no longer list it, though
the site map and the fatal-errors page still pointed here.

## Key takeaways

- Write down what happened right away. The site argued that memory and
  credibility decide many traffic trials.
- Go back to the scene, take your own photos, and collect weather and distance
  details that could test the officer's account.
- Identify both laws behind your ticket, the POA procedure and the offence
  section, and read the offence provision for its elements.
- Look for elements the prosecution might not prove, such as by-law proof for
  a community safety zone. The site used these as groundwork for disclosure and
  non-suit arguments in [Step 4](../guide/step-4-pre-trial.md) and
  [Step 5](../guide/step-5-trial.md).
- Case law research starts on CanLII. The site's own case citations date from
  ~2004–2006.
