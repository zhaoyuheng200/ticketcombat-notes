# Step 4 — Pre-trial strategies

*A summary of TicketCombat.com's Step 4 (pre-trial strategies) section: 28 pages, written ~2008–2011, Ontario. Not legal advice. Read the [original section introduction (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/20251103061123/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/step4.php).*

## What this section covered

Step 4 held most of the site's strategy. It covered the months between getting a notice of trial and the trial itself. The site redefined winning as avoiding a conviction, and it named three ways to get there: the charge is withdrawn, the justice stays the case, or you are acquitted. It argued that the first two could be set up before the trial started. Its two main tools were **disclosure** and **stays**. The tone is combative. The site described prosecutors as protecting their conviction numbers, and told readers to make their case so much work that the prosecutor would rather drop it. In the site's words, the aim was to "position your case to be not worth the risk for the prosecutor".

The disclosure pages build on the Crown's duty to disclose from **R v Stinchcombe, [1991] 3 SCR 326**. The site's advice was to write your own detailed request and follow up in writing, keep proof of every attempt, and use any gaps as grounds for an inadequate-disclosure argument. It also covered freedom-of-information requests under the **Municipal Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act**, interviewing witnesses, and when it was smarter not to ask for certain documents.

The stay pages base the remedy on **Charter s.24(1)** and list the rights the site saw raised most often in provincial offences cases: ss.7, 11(a), 11(b) and 11(d). A set of pages added between 2009 and 2011, which never made it into the site map, covered the filing procedure. That meant the **Courts of Justice Act s.109** notice of constitutional question (**Form 4F**), served on the Attorneys General of Canada and Ontario at least 15 days ahead, the provincial offences rules in **Regulation 200**, and three filing methods. These ranged from full in-person filing to raising the motion at trial with no notice at all. These pages keep complaining that clerks, prosecutors and justices applied the wrong court's rules to traffic matters.

The last part deals with **Charter s.11(b)**, the right to be tried within a reasonable time. The site called this the most common stay argument. It follows the **R v Askov (1990)** and **R v Morin (1992)** framework: waiver, length of delay, reasons for delay, and prejudice. It adds the site's own argument that court backlogs came from municipal choices, not real resource limits. All of this predates **R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27**, so these pages are flagged as dated below.

**Part 1 — Disclosure**

### Disclosure Section

`/step4/disclosure.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200708005521/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosure.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-07

Rests on R v Stinchcombe, [1991] 3 SCR 326: the Crown must share all relevant material, so the trial holds no surprises. The site gave three reasons to ask: to build a defence, to lay grounds for an inadequate-disclosure stay or adjournment, and because prosecutors withdraw more readily once they know you saw the gaps.

### How to request disclosure

`/step4/disclosurehow.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20250820191208/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosurehow.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-08

Mail or fax the request to the prosecutor's office at your trial court (address on the notice of trial). The prosecutor then asks the officer; most places make you collect the material. The site openly saw sweeping requests as a way to provoke non-response that could ground a dismissal. Its four-to-eight-week Toronto response time is from ~2008–2010.

- **Write your own detailed request** (template under [Downloads](#downloads)). The site called municipal fill-in forms, such as London's, a trap with no room to specify anything.
- **Set a return date and chase it:** about three weeks (a week of mail plus 15 days), with a written follow-up each time one lapses, to show active effort.
- **Keep proof:** fax confirmations, or hand delivery plus a free affidavit sworn at the courthouse.
- **Omit your phone number,** so excuses have to arrive in writing.

### What to ask for

`/step4/disclosurewhat.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024225/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosurewhat.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

**Part 2 — Stays**

### Stay Section

`/step4/stay.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203000902/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/stay.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

A stay simply halts the case: no verdict, no acquittal, no withdrawal. In effect the court grants a right not to be tried, meeting the goal of never being convicted. The Crown can appeal, but the site said that is rare and usually fails.

### How stays work, what rights can be violated

`/step4/works.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203001302/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/works.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Grounds a stay in Charter s.24(1), which lets the court pick the remedy (adjournment, costs or a stay), so you argue only a stay will do. The rights it saw raised most in provincial offences cases were s.7 (disclosure), s.11(a) (knowing the charge), s.11(b) (delay, the commonest) and s.11(d), which it tied to unfairly obtained evidence such as a breath demand without lawful grounds. It also named ss.14 and 15, and ss.8, 9 and 10(b) in criminal cases. The application must show when, where and how the right was breached.

**Part 2a — Filing a stay application** *(pages not in the site map)*

These nine pages were added between 2009 and 2011 and never appear in the site map. The site's sidebar placed them between *How it works* and the 11(b) pages, in the order below. They cover how to get a stay motion heard, not the arguments for it. They were written mostly with 11(b) delay applications in mind.

### Why stay procedure confuses people

`/step4/confusion.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024244/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/confusion.php) · first seen 2011-03, last captured 2020-02

Opens the filing pages. Ontario keeps separate rules for criminal, civil and regulatory matters, with traffic cases in the informal provincial offences courts. The site complained that clerks, and sometimes prosecutors and justices, wrongly impose civil or criminal requirements on tickets, and said knowing the simple real rules lets you correct them. It then sets the order: rules, filing methods ([How to apply for a stay](#how-to-apply-for-a-stay)), then the 11(b) arguments.

### Learn the rules

`/step4/learnrules.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204040922/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/learnrules.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Part VII of the Courts of Justice Act covers civil proceedings and shouldn't govern a ticket, but s.95(3) carries some of it into provincial offences courts, including s.109, which the site treated as the rulebook for stays.

### Section 109

`/step4/section_109.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204034952/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/section_109.php) · first seen 2011-03, last captured 2020-02

Walks through Courts of Justice Act s.109, the notice of constitutional question. It applies to a challenge to a law's validity or a Charter s.24(1) claim over a federal or Ontario government act or omission, and lets the Attorneys General of Canada and Ontario appear and oppose. The site reduced it to three duties: follow the court rules (Regulation 200, especially s.7 on motions), use the prescribed form, and give at least 15 days' notice. On counting, it applied Reg 200 s.4.4 (for an at-least period, neither end day counts) and s.4.3 (a deadline on a Saturday or holiday moves). Its worked example, a court date of Tuesday 24 May 2011 after Victoria Day, put the last filing day at 4 May, about 20 calendar days out. It also reported that the 2009 provincial appeal ruling in R v Vellone, the case Method 3 relies on, had held that s.109 didn't reach municipal prosecutions, and that the Ontario Court of Appeal reversed it in 2011 since the province can always take over a prosecution. Citing a Law Commission of Ontario proposal to require municipal notice, the site advised serving the municipal prosecutor too.

### The documents

`/step4/paperwork.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203035910/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/paperwork.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Describes the three documents for a stay application. The site argued that putting the case on paper suits nervous speakers and matches what the justice and prosecutor expect.

- **Form 4F**, the Notice of Constitutional Question, is the minimum. It goes to both Attorneys General (and, the site advised, the municipal prosecutor) and has room for your grounds; if they don't fit, a one-line note on the form points to an attached factum.
- **A factum** (optional) sets out how the case was handled and how your Charter rights were infringed. The site warned against using it to argue innocence, and said to append copies of any cases cited because justices dislike looking them up.
- **Affidavits** come in two kinds. One proves service (Form 16B, where you fill in only the section for your delivery method, or nothing if hand delivery got a stamp). The other states facts plainly, without opinion.

It links the site's own examples (see [Downloads](#downloads)), outside guides to factums and affidavits, and CanLII's Charter digest. It also notes that court clerks can usually commission affidavits for free.

### How to apply for a stay

`/step4/howtofile.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203105422/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Frames filing as a trade-off: each method is legally sound, but less advance work means more pushback from justices or prosecutors who don't recognise it. It covers only getting the motion heard, not its merits, and previews Method 1 (near-bulletproof, a day at court) and Method 2 (fax only).

### Method 1: filing in person

`/step4/howtofile_hard.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204034127/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile_hard.php) · first seen 2010-06, last captured 2020-02

The conventional route. It costs a day at the courthouse (and possibly lost wages) but means the justice finds everything in order at trial. You serve Form 4F, any factum and usually an affidavit of service on both Attorneys General, then on the prosecutor, then file with the court clerk. The site was blunt about clerks: describe the form and expect a blank stare, but tell them you are "filing an 11(b)", whatever your actual ground, and the door opens. It called downtown Toronto's set-up Kafkaesque. Swearing happened at 137 Edward Street, prosecutor intake was in the Old City Hall basement, and you went back to Edward Street to file, unless you swore before a justice at Old City Hall to save a trip. A prosecutor at 70 Centre Street might also deny ever receiving your papers. These locations are as of ~2010.

- Proof of service can be fax receipts, an affidavit of service for mailed copies (allow seven days plus postmark lag), or a signed service stamp on hand-delivered copies. A generic Received stamp doesn't count.
- In busy courts, take a number at the clerk's area before visiting the prosecutor's office.
- Swear affidavits before a clerk who is a commissioner of oaths to avoid notary fees, and keep stamped copies for trial.
- An affidavit of facts should be sworn before the prosecutor gets it. If made to visit the prosecutor first, hold it back and deliver a signed copy afterwards.
- If a clerk balks, don't argue: their job is to file, and the justice decides whether the papers are in order.

### Method 2: faxing

`/step4/howtofile_harder.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204042922/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile_harder.php) · first seen 2010-06, last captured 2020-02

Fax Form 4F (and any factum) to both Attorneys General, whose numbers are on the form, and to the prosecutor. Keep every receipt and file nothing with the court. The catch comes at arraignment ([Step 5](../guide/step-5-trial.md) covers when to speak up): the justice finds no motion on file, and the prosecutor may say no fax arrived. The site advised letting them finish, then replying very politely. The reply rests on the s.109 notice and receipts, on Reg 200 s.7(7), which allows a motion without notice where that isn't unjust, and on an offer to swear that the faxes were sent. It put the odds of a justice and prosecutor who knew these rules at under 20%.

### Method 3: a motion at trial with no notice

`/step4/howtofile_hardest.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20120213204454/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile_hardest.php) · first seen 2011-04, last captured 2012-02

Make the stay motion after arraignment with no notice. The site relied on R v Vellone, 2009 ONCJ 150 (paras 10–25), found by a reader and treated as binding appeal authority; bring three printed copies. It expected the prosecutor to ask for time to read the case. To resist, it suggested noting the procedure dated from at least 1999 and citing R v Zimmerman (2005, para 26) on fair, informal hearings for self-represented Charter applicants. If the extra time would strengthen an 11(b) claim, the site said to object only for the record, so no waiver could be inferred. It admitted faxing notice ahead (Method 2) is often more practical; Method 3's value was rescuing those who missed the 15-day notice. The site's own Section 109 page later reported that the 2009 Vellone ruling was overturned in 2011. This page was last captured in February 2012 and dropped out of the site's later navigation.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** the strategy of letting delay build up for an 11(b) claim predates R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27, which replaced the Askov/Morin framework with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### What to say in court

`/step4/goingtocourt.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204040427/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/goingtocourt.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Once the motion is accepted, walk the justice through it: they seldom read files beforehand, as many cases settle. Pause so they can read along, and point to the exact paragraphs of any case cited.

**Part 3 — Charter s.11(b): unreasonable delay**

### Understanding the 11b argument

`/step4/arguments.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024239/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/arguments.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Lists the factors the court balances under Morin: waiver, length of delay, the reasons for it, and prejudice. A long delay, even past ten months, plus an application forces that review, though the site said wins are hard.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** this factor-balancing test is the Askov/Morin framework, replaced by R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications - waiver of time periods

`/step4/waiver.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200202235007/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/waiver.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Waiver must be clear and unequivocal, so silence isn't one, though inaction may read as contentment. The site suggested arguing you reasonably assumed your charge was dropped, as many are. Accepting a trial date at first attendance or in a Part III case could hurt; its fix was the early-trial letter from [Step 2](../guide/step-2-request-a-trial.md) (`waiver.doc`).

> ⚠️ **Dated:** 11(b) delay is now governed by R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 (presumptive ceilings, 18 months in provincial court), not the Askov/Morin framework used here; the pre-trial meeting the site calls First Attendance is now an early resolution meeting, requestable online where the court offers it. See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications - length of delay

`/step4/length.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203040139/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/length.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Gives the guideline the site believed courts used: about 18 months from offence to trial, made of up to 8–10 months of intake (readying the case) and 6–8 months of institutional delay, and one part may run long if the total stays under. Custody, strict bail or real prejudice shortens it; a fine-only Part I case gets the full span. The core argument: the 10-month intake ceiling suits complex cases with wiretaps or experts, so a simple ticket needs about two months, leaving excess the prosecutor must justify. A 14-month speeding-ticket example works it through.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** this intake-plus-institutional guideline is from the Morin era; R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 replaced it with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications - reasons for the delay

`/step4/reasons.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203042852/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/reasons.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Covers the reasons for delay. Inherent time favours the accused, since a ticket usually rests on one officer's testimony. On institutional resources, the site said courts once examined backlogs closely, down to judges' hours, but had grown forgiving of supposedly temporary strain in fast-growing areas such as Markham, Milton and Brampton, swayed by Attorney General press releases. Its counter-argument was political. After the late-1990s amalgamations the province handed provincial offences courts, and the ticket revenue, to municipalities, which then wrote far more tickets without adding court capacity. It cited the 2008 provincial auditor's report: municipal ticketing up 57% over ten years, 78% in Toronto and 155% in Ottawa, against 20% for the OPP and 2% in Mississauga. It also pointed to Toronto ending night courts, cutting office hours, raising fines and dropping the 25% early-payment discount (all as of ~2008–2009). The page also covers:

- the community's interest in prompt and fair trials, which it said grows with the charge's seriousness (R v Payne, 2010);
- two judicial schools of thought on whether minor charges should move faster or slower than serious ones;
- the test for when Crown conduct counts as inherent delay: necessary, reasonable, meritorious and in good faith (R v Sander, 1995, BC Court of Appeal).

> ⚠️ **Dated:** this reasons-for-delay analysis belongs to the Askov/Morin framework, replaced by R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications – prejudice to the accused

`/step4/prejudice.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203110520/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/prejudice.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Explains that 11(b) protects the interests behind s.7 and s.11(d), and that some prejudice is presumed from delay alone and grows with time (Morin, per Sopinka J; R v Allen, 2009; R v Dehaney, 2005). The site still advised spelling it out, since a trial justice may not apply this. Part I offences carry no jail, so liberty arguments are weak, and it expected the prosecutor to steer the debate back to them. Instead readers were to focus on two things:

- security of the person, read broadly following Mills v The Queen (1986, para 145) to cover stigma, lost privacy, disruption to family and work, legal costs, uncertainty and the inability to plan, shown as worsening over time; dislike of the fine or an insurance increase doesn't count;
- harm to a fair trial, as memories fade and witnesses and evidence go missing, undermining full answer and defence.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** prejudice was a factor under the Askov/Morin framework, which R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 replaced with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

## Key takeaways

- The site defined winning as never being convicted, and used Step 4 to aim for a withdrawal or a stay before the trial started.
- It treated disclosure (Stinchcombe) as both a defence tool and a source of pressure: specific written requests, steady written follow-up, and proof of every attempt.
- Stays rested on Charter s.24(1). The filing procedure centred on a Courts of Justice Act s.109 notice (Form 4F) sent to both Attorneys General at least 15 days ahead, and the site advised notifying the municipal prosecutor too.
- The site kept warning that clerks, prosecutors and justices would get the procedure wrong. It told readers to bring copies of case law, extra documents, and proof of service.
- The 11(b) delay pages, precedents and templates come from the Askov/Morin era and predate R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27. Treat them as historical.
- Office addresses, phone numbers, response times and statistics are all as of when the pages were written (~2008–2011).
