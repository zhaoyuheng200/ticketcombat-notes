# Step 4 — Pre-trial strategies

*A summary of TicketCombat.com's Step 4 (pre-trial strategies) section: 28 pages, written ~2008–2011, Ontario. Not legal advice. Read the [original section introduction (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/20251103061123/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/step4.php).*

## What this section covered

Step 4 held most of the site's strategy. It covered the months between getting a notice of trial and the trial itself. The site redefined winning as avoiding a conviction, and it named three ways to get there: the charge is withdrawn, the justice stays the case, or you are acquitted. It argued that the first two could be set up before the trial started. Its two main tools were **disclosure** and **stays**. The tone is combative. The site described prosecutors as protecting their conviction numbers, and told readers to make their case so much work that the prosecutor would rather drop it, to "position your case to be not worth the risk for the prosecutor".

The disclosure pages build on the Crown's duty to disclose from **R v Stinchcombe, [1991] 3 SCR 326**. The site's advice was to write your own detailed request and follow up in writing, keep proof of every attempt, and use any gaps as grounds for an inadequate-disclosure argument. It also covered freedom-of-information requests under the **Municipal Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act**, interviewing witnesses, and when it was smarter not to ask for certain documents.

The stay pages base the remedy on **Charter s.24(1)** and list the rights the site saw raised most often in provincial offences cases: ss.7, 11(a), 11(b) and 11(d). A set of pages added between 2009 and 2011, which never made it into the site map, covered the filing procedure. That meant the **Courts of Justice Act s.109** notice of constitutional question (**Form 4F**), served on the Attorneys General of Canada and Ontario at least 15 days ahead, the provincial offences rules in **Regulation 200**, and three filing methods. These ranged from full in-person filing to raising the motion at trial with no notice at all. These pages keep complaining that clerks, prosecutors and justices applied the wrong court's rules to traffic matters.

The last part deals with **Charter s.11(b)**, the right to be tried within a reasonable time. The site called this the most common stay argument. It follows the **R v Askov (1990)** and **R v Morin (1992)** framework: waiver, length of delay, reasons for delay, and prejudice. It adds the site's own argument that court backlogs came from municipal choices, not real resource limits. All of this predates **R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27**, so these pages are flagged as dated below.

**Introduction**

### Step 4 introduction

`/step4/step4.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20251103061123/https://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/step4.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-11

After you ask for a trial, a notice of trial comes in the mail with a date anywhere from a few months to more than a year away. The site called this waiting period the most important part of your defence. It defined success as avoiding a conviction by withdrawal, stay or acquittal, and said Step 4 was about being in a position to win before the trial begins.

### Introduction to disclosures and stays

`/step4/friends.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20210502222044/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/friends.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2021-05

Introduces the two tools. Disclosure means asking the prosecutor for all of their evidence. A stay means asking the justice to stop the case, usually on Charter grounds. The site said few self-represented defendants use either one. It argued that someone who does forces the prosecutor to weigh the risk of losing, or of a ruling that affects other cases, against simply withdrawing the charge. It pointed to a 2004 Thunder Bay news story in which the city said ticket cases might be dismissed because staff couldn't keep up with evidence requests.

### An example of how it works

`/step4/example.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203065947/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/example.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Uses a Toronto case that was recent at the time (CBC News, April 2008). Drug-squad officers charged after a large RCMP investigation asked for disclosure of an enormous volume of evidence. When the disclosure dragged on for years, a judge stayed the charges. The site's lesson was that procedure, not guilt, often decides the outcome, and that ticket defendants should be just as willing to insist on their rights.

**Part 1 — Disclosure**

### Disclosure Section

`/step4/disclosure.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200708005521/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosure.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-07

Explains that the Crown must disclose everything relevant so there are no surprises at trial, citing R v Stinchcombe, [1991] 3 SCR 326. The site gave three reasons to ask for it:

- to prepare your defence;
- to set up an inadequate-disclosure argument, for a stay or an adjournment, if the prosecutor falls short;
- because prosecutors are more likely to withdraw once they know you have noticed the gaps.

### How to request disclosure

`/step4/disclosurehow.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20250820191208/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosurehow.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-08

Practical steps for sending a written request by mail or fax to the prosecutor's office at your trial court:

- **Use your own detailed request.** The site said not to use a municipality's own fill-in form, which it saw as a trap because the form has no space to say what you want. It gave London's form as the example.
- **Set a deadline and follow up.** It suggested a return date of about three weeks (a week for the mail plus 15 days), then following up in writing every time a date passed.
- **Keep proof.** Keep fax confirmations, or deliver the request in person and swear an affidavit that you did.
- **Leave your phone number off,** so that any reply has to come in writing.

The site was open that a very broad request might go unanswered, and that the non-response itself could become grounds for dismissal. Its estimate that Toronto took four to eight weeks to respond is as of when the page was written (~2008–2010).

### Toronto prosecutor addresses, phone and fax numbers

`/step4/torontoprosecutor.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024127/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/torontoprosecutor.php) · first seen 2010-12, last captured 2020-02

A short reference page. It is linked from the disclosure-request page and listed in the site map's sample-forms section, not under Step 4. It explains that the first four digits of a Toronto offence number tell you which prosecutor's office has the charge. It then gives the address, phone and fax for three offices: Old City Hall, Eglinton Avenue West, and the Markham Road court house. The addresses and numbers are from when the page was written (~2010).

### What to ask for

`/step4/disclosurewhat.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024225/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosurewhat.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Tells readers to be specific and not to accept a copy of the ticket as disclosure. The suggested minimum centred on the officer's complete notes and the witness material, plus certified copies of any records the charge depends on and anything else in the Crown's file. It also gave a tactical tip: don't ask for a by-law the charge relies on, such as one creating a community safety zone. The idea was that if the prosecution never proves the by-law, you can argue at trial that there is no evidence of it.

### What to do with what you get

`/step4/disclosureresult.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203000010/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/disclosureresult.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Connects disclosure to the Step 3 research on the essential elements of the offence ([Step 3 summary](../guide/step-3-preparation.md)). Readers were to go through the material to check that the Crown can prove each element and that proper procedure was followed. They should look for shortcuts or gaps that break the link between what was observed and the accused. The site's example was a roadside breath test given without the grounds for it being noted, which it said would make the reading inadmissible.

### Advanced disclosure techniques

`/step4/advanced.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200202234232/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/advanced.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Covers what to do when the prosecutor refuses a request as too large or irrelevant, or doesn't answer at all. The site saw this as a test of how much you know. It suggested offering to look through the material at the prosecutor's office to judge whether it's relevant, then asking for copies of specific pages. Radar and laser operating manuals were the typical case.

### Discovery

`/step4/discovery.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024122/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/discovery.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Despite the title, this page is about freedom-of-information requests. If material is ruled irrelevant for disclosure, the site said you could still ask the police services board for records under the Municipal Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act. Examples were training material, internal directives and policies, and details of equipment. You could also ask third parties. It linked a 2003 decision of Ontario's Information and Privacy Commissioner as an example.

### Interviewing witnesses

`/step4/interview.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204034727/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/interview.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Suggests contacting the witnesses named in disclosure to find out what they will say, sticking to factual questions. A reliable third person should be present, or the interview recorded with consent, so you can contradict a witness who changes their story at trial. The site said this was not the moment to argue your innocence. It warned that tampering with or threatening a witness is a serious criminal offence, and suggested inviting the prosecutor to attend.

**Part 2 — Stays**

### Stay Section

`/step4/stay.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203000902/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/stay.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Explains what a stay is. The proceedings simply stop, with no verdict either way and no withdrawal of the charge. In effect, the court grants you the right not to be tried. The site's point was that this reaches the real goal of never being convicted. It said the Crown can appeal a stay, but that this is rare and usually fails.

### How stays work, what rights can be violated

`/step4/works.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203001302/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/works.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Bases a stay on Charter s.24(1), which lets a court choose a remedy when a right is breached: an adjournment, costs, or a stay. The site said to argue that only a stay will do. The rights it saw raised most often in provincial offences cases were:

- s.7 (disclosure as a principle of fundamental justice);
- s.11(a) (being told what the offence is);
- s.11(b) (trial within a reasonable time, the most common);
- s.11(d) (which it connected to excluding unfairly obtained evidence).

It also mentioned ss.14 and 15, and ss.8, 9 and 10(b) for criminal cases. The application has to say when, where and how the right was breached, and why nothing short of stopping the case would fix it.

**Part 2a — Filing a stay application** *(pages not in the site map)*

These nine pages were added between 2009 and 2011 and never appear in the site map. The site's sidebar placed them between *How it works* and the 11(b) pages, in the order below. They cover how to get a stay motion heard, not the arguments for it. They were written mostly with 11(b) delay applications in mind.

### Why stay procedure confuses people

`/step4/confusion.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024244/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/confusion.php) · first seen 2011-03, last captured 2020-02

Opens the filing pages. Ontario has separate rules for criminal, civil, and regulatory (provincial offences) matters. The site complained that court clerks, and sometimes prosecutors and justices, apply civil or criminal requirements to traffic tickets. It said the real requirements are simple, and that knowing them lets you correct the court. It then sets out the order to follow: the rules, the filing methods ([How to apply for a stay](#how-to-apply-for-a-stay)), then the 11(b) arguments.

### Learn the rules

`/step4/learnrules.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204040922/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/learnrules.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Explains which law applies. Part VII of the Courts of Justice Act governs civil proceedings and generally shouldn't apply to a ticket. However, s.95(3) brings some provisions into provincial offences courts, including s.109 on constitutional questions. The site treated s.109 as the rulebook for stay applications. The Method 2 page sends readers back here for its in-court argument.

### Section 109

`/step4/section_109.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204034952/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/section_109.php) · first seen 2011-03, last captured 2020-02

Walks through Courts of Justice Act s.109, the notice of constitutional question. If you seek a Charter s.24(1) remedy against government action, or challenge whether a law is valid, you must notify the Attorneys General of Canada and Ontario. Notice must follow the court rules (Regulation 200, especially s.7 on motions), use the prescribed form, and arrive at least 15 days before the court date. Using a May 2011 example, the site showed that Reg 200's day-counting and holiday rules push the practical deadline to about 20 calendar days ahead. It also reported that a 2009 provincial appeal ruling had found s.109 didn't apply to municipal prosecutions, and that the Ontario Court of Appeal reversed it in 2011. This is the same R v Vellone decision that Method 3 relies on. The site recommended notifying the municipal prosecutor as well.

### The documents

`/step4/paperwork.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203035910/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/paperwork.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Describes the three documents for a stay application:

- **Form 4F**, the Notice of Constitutional Question. This is the minimum, and it goes to both Attorneys General (and, the site advised, the municipal prosecutor).
- **A factum** (optional), setting out the facts and the Charter argument. The site said it should not drift into arguing innocence.
- **Affidavits**, either of service (Form 16B) or of facts.

It links the site's own examples (see [Downloads](#downloads)), outside guides to writing factums and affidavits, and CanLII's Charter digest. It also notes that court clerks can usually commission affidavits for free.

### How to apply for a stay

`/step4/howtofile.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203105422/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Introduces the filing methods and stresses that this is only about getting the motion heard, not about its merits. The site framed it as a trade-off. The less work you do in advance, the more pushback you get at trial from a justice or prosecutor who doesn't recognise the method. The page describes two methods, filing in person and faxing. A third appears on its own page.

### Method 1: filing in person

`/step4/howtofile_hard.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204034127/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile_hard.php) · first seen 2010-06, last captured 2020-02

The conventional route. You serve Form 4F (and any factum) on both Attorneys General, then on the prosecutor, then file with the court clerk. Proof of service can be fax receipts, a sworn affidavit of service, or a signed service stamp; the site warned that a generic Received stamp is not enough. It takes roughly a day at the courthouse but means fewer arguments at trial. Tips included:

- tell the clerk you are "filing an 11(b)" instead of naming the form;
- swear affidavits before a clerk to avoid notary fees;
- follow a particular order of steps when an affidavit of facts is included.

A long passage describes how downtown Toronto split the steps between Old City Hall and Edward Street, with trials at 70 Centre Street. These locations are as of when the page was written (~2010).

### Method 2: faxing

`/step4/howtofile_harder.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204042922/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile_harder.php) · first seen 2010-06, last captured 2020-02

Fax Form 4F to both Attorneys General and the prosecutor, keep the receipts, and file nothing with the court. The site warned that the trouble comes at trial, when the justice finds no motion on file. It outlined a polite reply to make:

- the 15 days' notice under s.109 was given, and you have the receipts;
- Reg 200 s.7(7) allows a motion to be heard without notice where that would not be unjust;
- you are willing to swear that the faxes were sent.

It put the odds of getting a justice and prosecutor who knew these rules at under 20%.

### Method 3: a motion at trial with no notice

`/step4/howtofile_hardest.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20120213204454/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/howtofile_hardest.php) · first seen 2011-04, last captured 2012-02

Raise the stay motion after arraignment with no advance notice at all. The method relies on R v Vellone, 2009 ONCJ 150 (paras 10–25), which a reader had found, and on R v Zimmerman (2005) on fair, informal hearings for self-represented people bringing Charter applications. The site expected the prosecutor to ask for an adjournment to read the case. It said the extra time could strengthen an 11(b) claim, but still recommended objecting for the record so you can't be said to have waived your rights. It admitted Method 2 is often the more practical choice. The site's own Section 109 page later reported that the 2009 Vellone ruling was overturned in 2011. This page was last captured in February 2012 and is missing from the site's later navigation.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** the strategy of letting delay build up for an 11(b) claim predates R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27, which replaced the Askov/Morin framework with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### What to say in court

`/step4/goingtocourt.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200204040427/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/goingtocourt.php) · first seen 2009-09, last captured 2020-02

Once the motion is accepted, be ready to walk the justice through your application. The site said justices often haven't read the file in advance, because many cases settle or are adjourned. It advised pausing so they can read along, and pointing to the exact paragraphs of any case you cite. The in-court motion itself is covered in [Step 5](../guide/step-5-trial.md).

**Part 3 — Charter s.11(b): unreasonable delay**

### 11b argument precedents

`/step4/precedents.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20250815181250/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/precedents.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2025-08

Opens the 11(b) pages with the Charter's guarantee of trial within a reasonable time, which the site called the most common application in provincial offences courts. It lists nine decisions to read, from R v Askov (1990) and R v Morin (1992) through Ontario rulings up to R v Andrade (2011), and marks Morin and Andrade as the most important. Step 2's page on changing a trial date and the parking section ([parking tickets](../topics/parking-tickets.md)) also link here.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** these precedents come from the Askov/Morin era; R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 replaced that framework with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### Understanding the 11b argument

`/step4/arguments.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200220024239/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/arguments.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Sets out the Morin balancing test the court applied:

- any waiver;
- the total length of the delay;
- the reasons for it: what the case inherently needed, the conduct of each side, institutional resources, and other causes;
- prejudice to the accused.

The site said an unusually long delay, even something over ten months, together with an application, makes the court look at whether the delay was reasonable. It warned that winning is not easy.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** this factor-balancing test is the Askov/Morin framework, replaced by R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications - waiver of time periods

`/step4/waiver.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200202235007/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/waiver.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Explains that waiving your 11(b) rights has to be clear and unequivocal, so silence doesn't count, though doing nothing can be read as being content with the pace. The site suggested arguing that, since so many charges get dropped, it was reasonable to assume yours had been too. It warned that agreeing to a trial date in court, at first attendance or in Part III matters, could count against you. Its fix was the early-trial request letter recommended in [Step 2](../guide/step-2-request-a-trial.md), the site's `waiver.doc` template.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** 11(b) delay is now governed by R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 (presumptive ceilings, 18 months in provincial court), not the Askov/Morin framework used here; the pre-trial meeting the site calls First Attendance is now an early resolution meeting, requestable online where the court offers it. See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications - length of delay

`/step4/length.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203040139/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/length.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Describes the guideline the site understood courts to use: about 18 months from offence to trial. That was split into 8–10 months of intake (preparing the case) and 6–8 months of institutional delay, with less tolerance where the accused was in custody or otherwise prejudiced. The site's main argument was that a traffic ticket is simple, so intake should be short (it suggested about two months). Any time beyond that would be excess the prosecutor has to justify. A 14-month speeding-ticket example shows the arithmetic.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** this intake-plus-institutional guideline is from the Morin era; R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 replaced it with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications - reasons for the delay

`/step4/reasons.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203042852/http://www.ticketcombat.com/step4/reasons.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Covers the reasons for delay. On institutional resources, the site argued that courts had become too forgiving of temporary strain. It blamed backlogs on municipal decisions after the late-1990s amalgamations, when provincial offences courts were handed to municipalities that also kept the ticket revenue. It backed this with the 2008 provincial auditor's figures on rising ticket numbers and with Toronto's cuts to court services (all as of ~2008–2009). The page also covers:

- the community's interest in prompt trials (R v Payne, 2010);
- two judicial schools of thought on whether minor charges should move faster or slower than serious ones;
- the test for when the Crown's conduct justifies delay (R v Sander, 1995).

> ⚠️ **Dated:** this reasons-for-delay analysis belongs to the Askov/Morin framework, replaced by R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

### 11b applications – prejudice to the accused

`/step4/prejudice.php` · [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20200203110520/http://ticketcombat.com/step4/prejudice.php) · first seen 2008-12, last captured 2020-02

Explains that prejudice can be inferred from delay alone (Morin; R v Allen, 2009; R v Dehaney, 2005), but advised spelling it out anyway. Part I offences carry no jail, so the site considered liberty arguments weak. Instead, readers were to focus on two things:

- security of the person, read broadly following Mills v The Queen (1986) to cover stigma, disruption to family and work, costs, and uncertainty, but not simple worry about the fine or insurance;
- harm to a fair trial from fading memories and lost evidence.

> ⚠️ **Dated:** prejudice was a factor under the Askov/Morin framework, which R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27 replaced with presumptive ceilings (18 months in provincial court). See [What's changed](../whats-changed.md).

## Key takeaways

- The site defined winning as never being convicted, and used Step 4 to aim for a withdrawal or a stay before the trial started.
- It treated disclosure (Stinchcombe) as both a defence tool and a source of pressure: specific written requests, steady written follow-up, and proof of every attempt.
- Stays rested on Charter s.24(1). The filing procedure centred on a Courts of Justice Act s.109 notice (Form 4F) sent to both Attorneys General at least 15 days ahead, and the site advised notifying the municipal prosecutor too.
- The site kept warning that clerks, prosecutors and justices would get the procedure wrong. It told readers to bring copies of case law, extra documents, and proof of service.
- The 11(b) delay pages, precedents and templates come from the Askov/Morin era and predate R v Jordan, 2016 SCC 27. Treat them as historical.
- Office addresses, phone numbers, response times and statistics are all as of when the pages were written (~2008–2011).
